# Scale BB-D — supplementary material and reproduction package

Supplementary material for the ICA 2026 paper:

> *Capturing Long-Term Disease Trends through a Pandemic Shock: A Cause-Specific
> Extension of Scale BB Validated by Directional Accuracy*

**The paper itself is distributed officially by the ICA 2026 organisers and is
not reproduced here.** This repository holds the code, the input data and the
reference results behind it, so that every table of the paper can be traced,
checked and re-run. All local paths cited in the paper refer to this repository.

**Scale BB-D** carries the construction of Scale BB — the mortality improvement
scale published by the Society of Actuaries in 2012 — over from all-cause to
cause-specific mortality: two-dimensional smoothing of the age-by-year surface,
improvement rates read off the smoothed surface, and a blend of the end-of-data
improvement rate toward an explicit long-term rate *L* by a convergence year *P*.

## What the paper finds

The numbers below are regenerated by the code in this repository (`sex = total`).

- **Point forecasts (§5).** Over the 10-year window from cutoff 2014, Scale BB-D is
  within 4pp of the best simple baseline for four of eight series and ahead of it
  for one; it beats the best baseline in 2 of the 14 independent disease × sex
  cells. At 2–3-year horizons most of the gap is the starting level, not the trend.
  From a common starting level its trend and a log-linear slope differ by about
  1–1.6pp either way. At a fixed horizon no systematic fall of the error appears
  as the pandemic years enter the training window.
- **Direction (§6).** For the six series with a continuing decline, directional
  accuracy through the pandemic years is 56–92%, at or below the ex-post
  majority-direction benchmark of each window and no better than log-linear
  extrapolation. For validation windows that begin during the shock and run to
  2024, every trend method falls to 37–50% against a benchmark of 70–75%.
- **What the framework offers (§7–§8).** Not a better forecaster, but an explicit
  and governable trend assumption whose annual improvement rate is bounded: one
  common fit generates level, trend and catastrophe scenarios. For a death benefit on three causes, a trend shock
  moves the best-estimate liability by +32% at entry age 30 and +10–12% at age 60,
  against +9–11% at every age for the prescribed level stress.
- **The generational hypothesis (§3.4, §7.3).** A dedicated estimator of a pandemic
  cohort effect, applied to the 14 public cause × sex series, finds no consistent
  effect in the two post-shock years available. A power analysis over illustrative
  data conditions shows what decides detection: at a hypothetical intermediate
  exposure, aggregate rates in 5-year groups detect an effect that raises the later
  rate of the infected by 10% about one time in seven, while individual follow-up
  at the same exposure detects it about half the time after five post-shock years
  and in most cases after ten; a national incidence panel detects it in most cases
  after five years.

The study was designed for the **incidence rates of medical insurance** and a
generation-specific effect of the pandemic on them. Japan's open data contain
neither a cross-disease incidence panel nor individual follow-up of infection,
so the framework is developed and tested on cause-specific mortality: the
results of §5–§6 apply directly to death benefits contingent on specific
diseases, for which it is the assumption itself, and for incidence they
demonstrate the procedure. An insurer holding incidence and follow-up data can
run the same validation with
[`reproduction/backtest/run_own_data.py`](reproduction/backtest/) and the
cohort estimator with [`reproduction/cohort_power/`](reproduction/cohort_power/).

## What you can do here

| If you want to… | Go to |
|---|---|
| Re-run the backtest of §5–§6 from the raw statistics and check it against the paper | [`reproduction/backtest/`](reproduction/backtest/) — `bash run_all.sh` |
| Run the same validation on your own rate panel (incidence or claim experience) | `reproduction/backtest/run_own_data.py` |
| Re-run the BEL sensitivity demonstration of §8 | [`reproduction/bel_demo/`](reproduction/bel_demo/) — `bash run_all.sh` |
| Generate assumed-rate tables with the APC extension (§3.4, Appendix) | [`reproduction/generational/`](reproduction/generational/) |
| Apply the pandemic cohort-effect estimator and re-run the power analysis of §7.3 | [`reproduction/cohort_power/`](reproduction/cohort_power/) |
| Re-run the supplementary checks cited in §5, §6, §7, §8 and §10 | [`final/review/scripts/`](final/review/scripts/) |
| Look at the figures of the paper | [`figures/`](figures/) |
| See the numbers without running anything | `reproduction/backtest/reference_tables/`, `reproduction/bel_demo/reference_output_20260930/` |
| Check the data the study is built on | [`DATA_SOURCES.md`](DATA_SOURCES.md) |

Requirements: Python 3.10+ with `pandas`, `numpy`, `scipy`, `matplotlib`
(`openpyxl` for the BEL demonstration).

## Repository layout

```
scale-bb-d/
├── README.md               this file

    ├── bel_demo/           BEL sensitivity demonstration of §8

```

Section numbers (§) refer to the paper distributed by the ICA 2026 organisers.

Notes on the package READMEs: they record the history of the reference outputs
and name paths in the author's working repository (for example `ScaleBB/Research/`,
`ValidationTools/EAS/`, `fms_booster/`). Those paths, the older reference outputs
they mention (before 2026-09-03) and the vendor projection model used for the
reconciliation of §9.2 are not part of this repository. The Standard Life Tables
workbook used by one optional input check of `reproduction/generational/` is not
bundled (see `DATA_SOURCES.md`).
